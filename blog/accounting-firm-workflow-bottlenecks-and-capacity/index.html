How can an accounting firm see workflow bottlenecks before deadlines slip?

Use a small set of meaningful job statuses, owners, due dates and blocked reasons so managers can intervene before a deadline or client becomes the warning system.

26 August 20267 min readBy Run Lighter

Use a small set of job statuses that represent real handoffs, and require each active job to have one next-action owner, due date and blocked reason where relevant. Review exceptions and ageing by workflow stage, then confirm that status changes come from work evidence rather than manual dashboard grooming.

Many practices already have a job list and still cannot answer which work needs intervention today. One job says in progress even though it has waited for client information for three weeks. Another is allocated to a team member but needs partner review. A third looks healthy until the person who understands it takes leave.

A busier dashboard will not fix that. The underlying job states must mean something to the people doing and managing the work.

Treat status as a handoff, not a colour

Start with one repeated job type and follow completed examples from ready to closed. Name the few stages where responsibility, information or decision genuinely changes. For example: waiting for client information, ready for preparation, in preparation, ready for review, changes required, ready to lodge or deliver, and complete.

Each status needs an entry rule and an exit rule. Ready for review should mean the required work and supporting evidence are present, the preparer is named and known exceptions are recorded. It should not mean someone moved the card because a meeting is approaching.

Xero Practice Manager and MYOB practice-management products expose jobs, tasks, statuses, due dates, workload and reporting features. Those features can carry a useful operating model, but installing the software does not make the firm's status definitions reliable.

Add the minimum fields a manager can act on

For each active job, make these elements visible:

  • current workflow stage
  • one owner for the next action
  • the client or internal dependency blocking progress
  • due date and any earlier internal review date
  • time in the current stage
  • the reason for an exception or hold
  • who needs to decide when the normal path cannot continue

Avoid asking staff to maintain fields that do not drive a decision. If nobody uses a priority score, remove it. If every job is marked high priority, the field is not helping. The objective is a short exception list a manager can resolve, not a detailed duplicate of the work itself.

Separate workload from capacity

Workload is the amount and timing of assigned work. Capacity is the realistic ability of the team to complete it, considering skills, review bottlenecks, leave, client dependencies and deadlines. Hours alone can hide the constraint. Ten jobs waiting for one authorised reviewer are not solved by spare preparer hours.

Group work by the stage and capability that must move next. Look ahead by due date and expected effort, then surface concentrations: too much review due on one day, a recurring client-information delay, work allocated without the required skill or a manager owning too many unresolved exceptions.

A useful alert explains the condition and the required decision. Job 104 has been ready for review for four days and is due Friday is more actionable than capacity is red.

Connect status to evidence carefully

Some status changes can follow reliable events. When the approved document checklist becomes complete, a job may be prepared for a person to confirm readiness. When a review task is completed, the next action may be assigned. When an exception occurs, the workflow can stop the clock used for internal analysis or distinguish client waiting from internal delay.

Do not allow one email, uploaded file or time entry to advance work blindly. Confirm the event belongs to the right client, job and period, and keep the human approval point where completeness or professional work must be assessed.

Build an exception review, not a surveillance report

The daily or weekly review should answer a few questions:

  1. Which jobs are approaching a deadline without the next stage ready?
  2. Which jobs have stayed in one stage beyond the agreed normal range?
  3. Which owners or reviewers have more due work than their practical capacity?
  4. Which blocked reasons are repeated across many clients or job types?
  5. Which exceptions need a partner, manager or client decision now?

Use this to rebalance, escalate or improve the workflow. Do not turn status data into an unexplained staff-performance score. Ageing can reflect client delay, poor intake, system friction, training needs or a genuine complex case. The manager still interprets the evidence.

Test whether the view predicts trouble

Take several recently late or difficult jobs and ask whether the proposed statuses and rules would have shown the problem early enough to act. Include a job blocked by the client, a review queue, a rework loop, an absent team member and a genuinely complex matter.

Then run the view alongside the current management process. Compare it with what managers learn by asking the team. Fix false alarms and invisible exceptions. Automate notifications only after the underlying state is dependable.

Measure intervention and flow

Useful measures include time in stage, jobs approaching due date without a ready next step, work returned for changes, repeated blocked reasons and time from exception to decision. Capacity planning becomes more credible when the practice can see where work actually waits.
