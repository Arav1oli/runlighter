What happens during an on-site automation review?

A practical review follows one real workflow through the business, finds where work gets stuck and identifies one sensible place to start.

12 August 20266 min readBy Run Lighter

Short answer

An on-site automation review follows one real workflow through your business, identifies repeated work and handover friction, separates rules from judgement and recommends one practical place to start.

It is not a tour of AI tools and it is not a promise to automate everything. The useful work is diagnostic. We follow what actually happens when a client, document, approval or report moves through the business, including the awkward cases that do not match the procedure manual.

For an established accounting, legal, advisory, recruitment, property, insurance or healthcare administration business, that usually means watching where skilled people spend time moving information, checking status and recovering handoffs that should have been clear.

Start with one real piece of work

The review begins with a recent example, not a hypothetical process map. It might be a new client who signed but waited for onboarding, an approved scope change that did not reach billing, a monthly report rebuilt from several systems or a document approval that sat in an inbox without a clear owner.

We identify the trigger, the intended outcome and the person accountable for the result. Then we trace every step in between. Where did the information first arrive? Who touched it? What was copied? Which systems changed? How did the next person know it was their turn? What happened when information was missing?

This matters because the official process and the real process are often different. A procedure may say that a task is assigned immediately. The team may know that assignment depends on someone noticing an email, opening a spreadsheet and sending a message. Automation should be built around the real path, not the neat version that exists only on paper.

Watch the handoffs, not just the tasks

The most expensive friction often sits between tasks. One person finishes their part, but the next person cannot see the decision, the required document or the current status. Work waits, gets chased or is reconstructed from old messages.

During the review, we look for repeated handoffs such as:

  • an enquiry copied from a form into a CRM and then into a task list
  • a client approval that does not update delivery or billing
  • a document waiting because the responsible reviewer is unclear
  • a report rebuilt from systems that use different definitions
  • an exception that returns to the owner because nobody else can see the rule

The goal is not to blame the person doing the recovery work. They are usually keeping the business moving despite a weak system. The review makes the dependency visible so the business can decide whether a clearer process, an existing software setting or a small automation would remove it.

Separate repeatable rules from human judgement

A useful review does not treat every decision as a rule. It asks which steps should happen consistently and which steps genuinely require experience, context or responsibility.

Routine information movement is often suitable for automation. A system can capture approved details, create a record, notify an accountable person, prepare a document, surface an overdue task and preserve an audit trail. Those steps are precise and repeatable.

Advice, pricing, qualification, unusual commitments, relationship decisions and final approval should normally remain with the responsible person. The review documents that boundary before anything is built. If the rules change every week or the team cannot agree on the expected outcome, the right recommendation may be to stabilise the process first.

That same principle applies to client scope and billing handoffs and lead follow-up after advertising. Automation can carry the repeated path, but commercial judgement stays human.

Rank the bottleneck by commercial consequence

Not every irritating task deserves a build. The review ranks opportunities by consequence, frequency, clarity and practicality. A repeated handoff that delays client work, hides revenue, weakens follow-up or consumes skilled attention usually matters more than a novelty automation that saves a few clicks.

We also check the exception path. What happens when a document is incomplete, the usual owner is away, a client asks for something outside scope or a system is unavailable? A workflow that only handles the perfect case can create more recovery work than it removes.

The strongest first project is usually narrow enough to test with real examples and important enough that the team will notice the improvement. It should have a known trigger, a clear owner, a normal path, an exception path and an outcome that can be checked.

Choose one practical place to start

The review should leave you with a clearer process and a specific next move. That may be a small change to the software already in place, a revised responsibility, a short automation build or a decision not to automate yet. Replacing the whole software stack is not the default.

A practical starting recommendation explains:

  • the workflow and commercial problem being addressed
  • what begins the process and what success looks like
  • which systems and people are involved
  • which steps can run automatically
  • which decisions stay with people
  • how exceptions will surface
  • how the change can be tested before wider use

If a larger build is sensible, this evidence makes the scope more credible. If it is not, the review should say so. The aim is to reduce uncertainty before the business commits time or money.

What to bring and what you leave with

Bring one recent example, access to the people who understand the work, the systems involved and the most common reason the process goes off track. That is enough to begin. You do not need a finished process map or a shopping list of tools.
